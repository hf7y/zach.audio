Essay

Cadaver Synod

I cannot begin to develop my sense of the academic composer without first taking on the story of the discipline’s biggest champion, Milton Babbitt. His story places us in a distinctly post-war, American context. At this moment, a prospering population could purchase music from the maturing recording industry. In a country where the customer is always right, these Americans had no need for composers like Babbitt whose “specialized” and “anachronistic”1 habits had alienated him from the vast and ever-growing majority of listeners; ambitious individuals were always vying to provide listeners with other music they would rather hear instead. In this context, we can already see the two main anxieties that haunt academic composers to this day: economic insecurity and a crisis of legitimacy in the face of dwindling audiences.

In response to these anxieties, Babbitt’s strategy, famously, was to retreat into the ivory tower.23 As composer/theorist and Babbitt hagiographer Robert Morris reminisces, “[b]y instituting a Ph.D. program in composition/theory at Princeton in the late 1950s, Babbitt heralded a role for composers within the academic community, where one could pursue what was analogous to research and development in other fields, like engineering and medicine.”4 This new role for composers took the sting out of those economic and reputational anxieties. A “pleasant and profitable”5 faculty position could guarantee the academic composer something of the middle-class life with which he had been raised and which he had come to expect for himself. Moreover, the prestige of these elite institutions would distinguish the academic composer from other “vanity” composers whose lesser-quality music enjoyed audiences of approximately the same size as his own. Within the academy, the composer was safe-guarded from the precarity and debasement of the marketplace.

Note 2

Babbitt’s objectivist premise is nearly that being free from the commercial market liberates him. It’s true that some forces get diffused by the ivory tower move, but it’s clear now that other incentives are just as strong, only different. Academic composers write for a prestige economy. Social and community bands write for their community. Market forces are just some forces.

But Babbitt’s “heralding” depended on an analogy between music and the sciences that is, in many ways, not obvious. To persuade his Princeton colleagues and others, Babbitt invoked a notion of “serious music,” a term that should instantly raise suspicion. The idea was that “serious music” is music concerned with progress, innovation and other modernist keywords like discovery and evolution. Like the atomic physicist whose research, once thought to be esoteric and useless, had helped the good guys win the war, the composer of serious music would make discoveries whose profit for society could only be known generations later.

Of course, Babbitt’s “serious music” was a codeword.6 It was invoked at the exclusion of all those “other” musics: provincial music, Black music, even the European-sounding music of the cinema. The idea that a society left only with these non-“serious” musics would not evolve or that music would “cease to live”7 should seem absurd to readers today (if not classist, racist and generally unimaginative). We have done well as a discipline to the extent that we have abandoned such an exclusive and hostile self-image. Yet the fact remains that academic composers like myself and my colleagues exist solely due to the privileges won for us by these very claims. Having disabused ourselves of Babbitt, we are faced yet again with the task of justifying our residency within the ivory tower.

Retrograde Inversion

Moving beyond Babbitt’s notion of “serious music” is, of course, easy to do now that the retreat into academia by American composers is a fait accompli. As Robert Morris reminds us, in the wake of Babbitt, “almost all American composers today, no matter how eminent in the musical marketplace, have academic appointments in composition or music theory.”8 Having won the war, we can and we should dispose of our dubious proschemata.

Note 8

Morris, “What Milton Babbitt Enabled,” 19–21. See bibliography: Morris 2012.

In my experience, this has largely happened. Though some composers still imagine themselves to be marching in the one true intellectual vanguard, many of us are satisfied to be writing music in conversation with our peers and to be caring for our students. Maintaining that special position atop the musical hierarchy would require us to deploy rhetorical means to keep the Beatles and Beyonce underfoot, a not particularly appealing project to those of us with more relativist sensibilities. Besides, our ethnomusicologist colleagues would never let that pass.

Given this status quo, I will take the opportunity to invert Babbitt’s argument. Babbitt makes a case for a “special music”9 deserving of special privileges. I argue the opposite: that the special privileges academic composers enjoy today give us the power, and the responsibility10, to make special music.11121314 Congruent with the anxieties listed earlier, these privileges center around economic freedom (from the “marketplace of the concert hall”15) and a related freedom to choose the critics we take seriously (“specialists” as opposed to “laymen”16).

In a sense, the idea is that one should not need to be a master to have influence. However the structure may still be that it is the masters who possess the agency. With Cheng, the benevolent masters themselves receive information about the effects of the communication on its recipients. My answer is a liberal one: good for others by giving them agency to choose. In their free choice, what they choose tells us about what they think is good. This is naive and anti-paternalistic. I’m open to balancing it slightly. As an educator, obviously on some level I know better than my students; that’s why they take my classes. But even still, I aspire to not simply tell them what’s good; rather, I try to give them the tools to determine for themselves what’s good. This is why I like amateurism, though. The amatuer who loves (amore) shows us what is worth doing through the act of doing it. Then there’s Steven Brown with Galbraith where in the Social and Political side of the flow chart senders and recipients are often of the same population. He mentions how in direct transmission of music, often it’s the same people on both sides. In contrast, with the industrial model, senders are specialists and receivers are consumers. So the insight here is about persuasion. What music persuades its recipients to become senders? From first principles, we know that recipients must be technically capable of becoming senders, of producing the message. They can be trained to do so. I imagine friction is least where the least training is required here. If the medium were music, the recipients must include musicians. Recipients of multimedia works can include a greater diversity of talents. Recipients walk out being able to send the message. Anthems are great examples of music that reproduces itself. The Anthem for Eris notably starts where performers may choose their voice number, playing from an <0126> tetrachord. No rules effectively determine who plays what. Sections may confer and count off numbers, but if someone were to switch it would be hardly detectable. In fact the <0126> set is dissonant enough that any “wrong note” would be difficult to hear anyway. Thus the Anthem for Eris embodies the political principles that organize the group itself. Eris is also anarchic. Everyone is a sender seeking to persuade in Eris logic. The social function of music must be to benefit the senders via identification and adaptation. The directed stimulation and association must communicate that benefit, and what’s persuasive to the receivers is the appeal of that benefit such that the receivers desire to become senders in some way or another. Identification is a strong force of motivation because exclusion is incredibly painful. But closing the loop means linking back to being motivated. It’s that the music creates the motivation for more of itself. You have music that creates something like a brand or an identity, a scene, a type of person. People identify with that. Then their identification demands more of the thing they use to identify that sharpens over time. The audience develops an aesthetic that certain artistic developments resonate more with certain crowds. That gets fed back into the artists who make the material to give it back to the people. That’s the closed loop. I’m interested in things like loops that aren’t purely just aesthetic, ritual loops, loops that talk about the passage of time, or that are productive in ways so that things ride on the loop. For example, communities that do charity activity, even though the core loop is recreational, that’s the social aid and pleasure club. The pleasure makes the loop. The social aid rides the pleasure. I’m a magician, and I’m steering. The irony is that the real magic is never what I was trying to perform. In a twist, the works at their best surprise me. A magician, I witness an illusion I didn’t intend, I didn’t perform, and my insider status as the one who knows the tricks gets upended; suddenly I’m left wondering if magic is real afterall. In this way, despite my steering, I am never steering when it comes to the big magic that transcends illusion. That’s always a deus ex machina. And I want to be a neutral steward, but I understand that my craft, my leadership, my magic tricks are needed to initially bend the energy into the closed loop. My hypothesis, the hope, is that one we kick-start the engine, it can hum without me. As the loop repeats, it can make aesthetic progress etc. That’s the difference between an engine and a bomb. The bomb uses all its fuel at once and is more about finality than continuity. I know that only when it’s humming without me does the real magic happen. These experiences have also led to my belief that composers are therefore responsible for creating the social dynamics we want to see in the broader world. The idea is to create works that come alive and continue development out of my hands. It’s an ethic. It creates a micro-polity. It’s an approach to writing music that gets us out of the object logic of mechanical reproduction, the commodity form? My bet is on what I know lasts: civic ritual, banding together, social aid and pleasure. See Appendix A. See Appendix B. See glossary: direct transmission. See glossary: identification. In making this argument, I am following philosopher Lydia Goehr in her essay “Political Music and the Politics of Music.”19 Goehr synthesizes a range of largely European philosophers and theorists who have taken on this paradox of situating music in relation to society. To roughly summarize her position, Goehr arrives at a notion of “freedom within,” that is, music within society.20 The idea is that music is not simply the inevitable result of social forces (popular demand, for example). At the same time music cannot be truly “transcendent” (a slippery term) in isolation from society. Goehr herself admits that this description resists clarity. Still, to the extent that we believe such a “transcendent” or “special” music is possible, Goehr’s might be the best road map we have.

I bring in this road map here because it leads us to two useful positions. One regards how we might situate the freedom of the academic composer in relation to Goehr’s “freedom within.” The other is how we might use composition to pick up where Goehr, as a philosopher, is forced to leave off.

Goehr contemplates the various social conditions under which music might manifest its transcendent potential. She lays out two extremes. On the one hand, “[m]usic is most threatened when it has to fight against a society that, in its own unfreedom, denies music its freedom.”21 Conversely, in a description that should remind us of the university, music is allowed to flourish in a “society that allows domains of freedom to exist within it, and allows music to occupy one of those domains in music’s essential state as at once separate but connected to society”22 Goehr leaves open the possibility that either extreme might manifest “transcendent” music more readily. Suffice it to say, academic composers likely prefer the “healthy antagonism” that we currently enjoy. To the extent that we believe in “special” music, we must believe in our own power to create it.

The second place Goehr leaves us is at the limit of philosophy. Throughout her essay, Goehr struggles with the ability of language to detail the dynamic between music and society that she is exploring. To her credit, Goehr does not insist that the limit of her philosophical approach is the limit of our understanding. Goehr quotes Wittgenstein, another philosopher who came up against the limits of his method, as saying “whatever the experience is, it is not cognitive.” As practitioners, we should be happy where this leaves us. No philosopher can tell us how “special”music is really made; we must be the ones to make it.

Ending on a High Note

Goehr’s philosophical stance reminds me of one of her contemporaries, one of my personal favorite philosophers, Richard Rorty. Rorty wrote extensively on the inability of philosophy to form the foundations of our values. For him, values came first; language was just a means to reason towards those ends that we have already chosen for ourselves.23 That’s where this mix of Babbitt and Goehr leaves us. As academic composers we are free—perhaps freer than any others—to imagine and enact our transcendental vision. I claim that, in what might just be comic-book naivete, that is what we must be concerning ourselves with.

To conclude, I will introduce a debate transpiring just down the halls of the academy in musicology. In the introduction to his monograph, “Just Vibrations,” William Cheng characterizes the state of his discipline. Authors operate under a paradigm of “paranoid reading”; they write to advance their careers and secure their privilege. To this Cheng asks “as we scramble for authority—as we race for erudite discoveries and prestige—should we pause to check our moral pulse[?]”24 Cheng, too, imagines what might be possible if those empowered within the academy collectively a new responsibility. For him, this is the duty of care. “Academic freedom,” he writes, “isn’t a license to be carefree. It’s an opportunity to care widely, assertively, and generously.”25 Cheng calls on his colleagues to “envision musicology as all the activities, care, and caregiving of people who identify as members of the musicology community.”2627 If musicologists can’t achieve this, Cheng argues, nothing else matters.

If musicologists (as bound as they are by truth-telling and rationality) can arrive at this position, academic composers, in our “freedom from” won for us by the Babbitt’s, surely can too. I won’t go so far as to say that this caregiving is precisely the utopian vision Goehr suggests characterizes the transcendental; we’ve already established the limits of language on that front. Rather, I am suggesting that the same freedom that enables us to quest for a “special” music enables us to manifest, in tangible ways, a vision for ourselves based on more than “highly sophisticated and complex constructive methods”28 that alienate us from our other communities. Of course, I cannot win this argument with language; I have to settle for inspiration. In a radio interview, Rorty put it bluntly: “you just decide what kind of future you want for humanity and go on from there.”29

Appendix C: Rorty against Wark

1. Two makers of new words

Rorty’s notion of the strong poet perhaps relates to innovation in directed stimulation as it relates to directed association. Rorty’s strong poet is Wark’s hacker, one who innovates in directed stimulation to reinforce certain associations (coinages) such that new communications become persuasive. The recipient has become the sender, though with much less sophistication. That’s Rorty’s strong poetry at its catchiest.

See glossary: directed stimulation. See glossary: directed association. See bibliography: Rorty 1989. See bibliography: Wark 2004.

2. Who keeps the coinage

Music, like poetry, is a hack in that it can be freely produced and reproduced, or nearly so. The regime cannot be one of intellectual property, that legible asset vectoralists seek to enclose and extract rents from. The regime must be the free production of the hacker, the anarchist logic that post-scarcity information naturally operates under. Whether they become enclosed is an open question. At the least I would credit my players as composers alongside myself.

3. The composer’s coinage

Notation is programming. Notation is basically software for the Music and Manipulation machine.

See bibliography: Brown 2006.

4. Persuasion as the exhortation to care

The persuasive component of the communication is the exhortation to care, to buy in to the social and political function. That’s Rorty actually. What music do individuals in their freedom choose to reproduce, and why?

See glossary: care. See bibliography: Rorty 1989.
