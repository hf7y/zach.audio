Feldman on notation as a second pass: his words and the sketch scholarship

What prompted this (Zach, chat 2026-11-27, verbatim): "the notation path needs either a human angle (these are easier for humans to underestand) or an interesting composer angle (these are sounds, these are approaches, something). Perhaps we can analyze some Feldman pieces with the question of whether his accidentals and dots and flags were added after an initial draft of composing. so dots and flags are part of a second-pass style in known compositions"

The question as briefed: did Feldman decide rhythmic notation (dots, flags, ties, meters) and his enharmonic spellings as a pass after the pitch material, and what did he say notation of duration does?

0. Answer in brief (mine)

  • Open on the public record, with partial support. No published sketch study the agent could open shows pitch written first and rhythm added later. The nearest evidence is three things: Feldman describing meters as something he gives to modules already written (Volans, 1984); Feldman telling students to "Spell it one way, then spell it another way... Use this kind of rhythm and then use another kind" (Middelburg, 1985); and a pianist who compared copies at the Sacher Stiftung finding spellings, octave signs and grace-note figures changed between copies, "as if, on re-writing the score... Feldman re-hears it, almost re-composes it" (Thomas).
  • Against a strict two-pass picture: Feldman said he wrote in ink straight into the score and left the piece when he found himself crossing out (Zimmermann, 1976); a reminiscence has him playing a chord at the piano and writing it into the final score (search-confirmed). For The Viola in My Life he said the rhythm came out of a sound (the muted viola's crescendo).
  • What his words do establish: he treated rhythmic notation as partly a thing for the eye and the player rather than the ear: "notational images that do not make a direct impact on the ear", "rhythm is notation, many times", "On paper it looks as though it were rhythm. It's not. It's duration." Analyses show the same pitch material written with different meters or durations (For Bunita Marcus pp. 24-25; String Quartet 1979 phrase; Triadic Memories). But the strongest cases of "identical sound, different notation" are few and named only by performers (Thomas, Pritchett, Sabat), mostly without bar numbers.

1. Feldman's own statements

1a. "Crippled Symmetry" (1981)

First published in Res 2 (Autumn 1981), pp. 91-103; reprinted in *Give My Regards to Eighth Street*, ed. B. H. Friedman (Exact Change, 2000), pp. 134-149. The essay itself was **not opened** (the only online copy found, on Scribd, is reported as badly garbled OCR). Everything below is as quoted by others.

  • The notational image. Pritchett quotes the first half, citing GMR p. 143: "Though these patterns exist in rhythmic shapes articulated by instrumental sounds"; Linson quotes the rest, citing Res p. 97: "they are also in part _notational images_ that do not make a direct impact on the ear as we listen." Both quote the next sentence: "A tumbling of sorts happens in midair between their translation from the page and their execution." [read, via Pritchett https://musicofjohncage.com/2016/04/28/triadic-memory-4-rhythms-structures/ and Adam Linson, "Through the Membrane", ECHO 4, 2023, https://echo.orpheusinstituut.be/article/through-the-membrane; the join of the two halves is mine]
  • On how exactly to notate them: "If notated exactly, they are too stiff; if given the slightest notational leeway, they are too loose" (Res p. 97). [read, via Linson; also quoted by Philip Thomas and by Pritchett on For Bunita Marcus]
  • Notation's weight: "the almost hierarchical prominence I attribute to the notation's effect on composition" (GMR p. 144). [read, via Tom Hall 2007, n. 3, below]
  • Graph paper, looking back at 1951: "I put sheets of graph paper on the wall; each sheet framed the same time duration and was," / "in effect, a visual rhythmic structure." / "the horizontal squares of the graph paper" / "represented the tempo—with each box equal to a preestablished ictus; and the vertical squares were the instrumentation of the composition." (GMR p. 147) [read, via Michael Gallope's commentary in the Getty Scores Project, https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/01/commentary/]
  • Bar lines in the String Quartet (1979): "The rhythmic structure of the block consists of four uneven bar lengths with four permutations that incorporate the instrumentation of the quartet. I must caution the reader not to take the bar lines here at face value. This passage becomes rhythmically obscured by the complicated nonpatterned syncopation that results. Only after rehearsals, and by following the score, could I catch an individual pattern as it crisscrossed from one instrument to another." [read, as reproduced in Tim Pierson, "What is Symmetry" (exhibition text), https://cdn.contemporaryartlibrary.org/store/doc/75654/docfile/c165de6d2d591d7407dea961ef9a2d4d.pdf; the last sentence also in Hall, citing GMR p. 141]
  • Spring of Chosroes: "The use of three pitches against five uneven beats created, in my ears, a crippled symmetric constellation of “eight” as I was writing it." [read, same Pierson text]
  • Meter as container: "For my purpose, [an asymmetric duration proportion] “contains” my material more within the metric frame of the measure; while in post-Webern arrhythmic language, lopsided acceleration results from the directional pull of one figure to another." (GMR p. 135; bracket is Jurkowski's) [read, via Edward Jurkowski, "Aspects of time in the later music of Morton Feldman", Musicological Explorations 7 (2006), 91-114, https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/bitstreams/85765ea6-407b-473c-a449-696db6e00542/download]

1b. Interviews and lectures (transcripts hosted at rohandrape.net, from Chris Villars's texts)

  • Walter Zimmermann, Desert Plants (1976), pp. 1-20 [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Zimmermann1976b.pdf]:
  • "So, the reason my music is notated is I wanted to keep control of the silence, you see. Actually, when you hear it, you have no idea rhythmically how complicated that is on paper. It's floating. On paper it looks as though it were rhythm. It's not. It's duration."
  • On writing in ink: "So if I begin to work and I see that I am crossing out all the time, I realize in a sense that I thought I was concentrated, but in fact I wasn't concentrated. So the writing in ink is an inner parameter to how concentrated I really am."
  • Note: Dierk Zaiser (Hamburg University Press, 2025, pp. 61-66, https://hup.sub.uni-hamburg.de/oa-pub/catalog/download/276/chapter-07/1834?inline=1) quotes the silence/duration passage "(Feldman in Sani 2000, S. 7)" as said "in direktem Zusammenhang mit seinem Piano and String Quartet". The interview is from 1976, nine years before that piece; cite Zimmermann, not Sani. [read]
  • Kevin Volans, conversation, Darmstadt, July 1984 (the day after the Darmstadt lecture) [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Volans1984a.pdf]. The closest thing in the record to Feldman describing meter as a later act:
  • "I mean, rhythm is notation, many times, outside of the fact what you want to do with it. So what I'm doing now is having small modules, very simple formations, very simple. Say one system, there are nine measures. And I'm writing, it's flute, percussion and piano. And I look at it and I give it ... the first five measures are in one-two, and then there are four measures of three-eight. Some of them begin, maybe, four measures, three-eight, another one begins five-eight."
  • "I would use four measures, for example. Okay. Seven-two, four double-dotted; seven-four, four double-dotted; seven-eight, four double-dotted; and seven-sixteen." Then: "I would say, maybe seven-sixteen is too fast. It would make for syncopation. And then I kind of make quick ... I do one measure and I see what the formulation is, and you don't even hear it."
  • Mine: four double-dotted notes fill 7/x exactly (a double-dotted value is 7/4 of its plain value), so this is one figure written at four levels of diminution, each a halving of the last. "one-two" is presumably 1/2 or 2/2; the transcript does not say.
  • On Crippled Symmetry parts: "very strict individual parts, which were calculated all the time on just a cheap calculator. In other words, after I finished two or three pages, I then would want to see how many eighth-notes ... and I would see that the flute is maybe a hundred and fifty eighth-notes behind".
  • "That's another translation I do: rhythm - pacing - duration - breathing."
  • Cole Gagne and Tracy Caras, Soundpieces (1982), pp. 164-177 [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Gagne1982a.pdf]:
  • "Notation is an aspect of style. And I find that if you use a certain type of notation, it cannot help but develop into a certain style."
  • "But precise notation slows it down a little bit. Just enough. Like doing 55 on the highway."
  • "That is what notation is to composition. How you notate determines more about the piece than any kind of system using this or that."
  • On the arc: "I never really "returned" to traditional notation. If you ever look at my list of works, I always alternated between one and the other." And: "I found that my most far-out notation repeated historical cliches in performance more than my precise notation. Precise notation is my handwriting."
  • Jan Williams, Percussive Notes, Sept. 1983, pp. 4-14 (interview 22 April 1983) [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Williams1983b.pdf]:
  • "I still use a grid. But now the grid encompasses conventional notation."
  • The first graph piece, Projection I: "I gave high, middle and low and each box corresponded to a metronome beat. At that time it was 72 which was very slow then."
  • "One of the problems I had with the early grid is that there was a tendency for it to be too design-oriented. It was very easy to make wonderful designs on the page, which I did."
  • Nothing in this interview on dots, flags or meters.
  • Toronto lecture, 17 April 1982 (transcribed by Linda Catlin Smith), on *Flute and Orchestra* and string writing [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Feldman1982a.pdf]:
  • "this was the beginning of new concerns for me, different spellings, and what sounded out of tune, in the strings were not out of tune, it was the way the instruments were spelled".
  • "I would take double sharps and double flats very seriously but not in a Schonbergian kind of leading tone way, but it was still related to the pitch - but as you know a sharp is a little more directional. I started to take spelling very, very seriously, and to me it was like adding a little turpentine in the chromatic field."
  • On the piece for John Cage: "you know how difficult it is to write a complicated rhythm that doesn't even sound like a rhythm? Try it."
  • Johannesburg lecture "Feldman on Feldman", August 1983 (transcribed by Rüdiger Meyer) [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Feldman1983a.pdf]:
  • "I'm involved with breathing, which I don't consider rhythm, but I have to measure it in time and the actual rhythm which I'm really hearing as rhythm."
  • On a flute theme that "spelt out B A C H": "it was just that when I modulated it for the flute it happened to be B A C H." (Pitch letters as a by-product of transposition.)
  • Working title for a piece: "Don't compose, try things out." ... "But there's no such thing, once you're trying something out on paper, you're composing."
  • "To Have Known Stefan Wolpe" (Buffalo, 1983) [read, full text, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Feldman1983c.pdf]: "It was the basis of my graph music. For example: the time is given but not the pitch. Or, the pitch is given and not the rhythm."
  • Everett Frost (in Bryden ed., Samuel Beckett and Music, 1998, pp. 47-55) [read, https://rohandrape.net/ut/rttcc-text/Frost1998a.pdf]: on Words and Music, "I created a composite line of the first line to my scale... It was only the first line that gave me his rhythm and pacing." Nothing on dots, flags or meters.
  • Middelburg, 1985 (Morton Feldman in Middelburg: Words on Music, ed. Raoul Mörchen, MusikTexte 2008, pp. 456-458), as quoted by Marc Sabat (below) [read, via Sabat]: "Okay. So do it one way and do it another. Spell it one way, then spell it another way. Orchestrate it one way, orchestrate it another way. Use this kind of rhythm and then use another kind." And on the violin: "the only way to do that is to say that it could be spelled D, C double sharp, or E double flat." And (p. 614): "So I might want to go to the cracks and fill it in a little bit with turpentine."
  • The Viola in My Life, Feldman's note [search-confirmed; Universal Edition page https://www.universaledition.com/en/Works/The-Viola-in-My-Life-1/P0037595 returned 403]: "I needed the exact time proportions underlying the gradual and slight crescendo characteristic of all the muted sounds the viola plays. It was this aspect that determined the rhythmic sequence of events." The Hollywood Bowl note [read, https://www.hollywoodbowl.com/musicdb/pieces/4496/viola-in-my-life-nos-1-and-2] has: "The Viola in My Life is conventionally notated as regards pitches and tempos."
  • "Liner Notes" (1962), GMR p. 6 [read, via Getty commentary]: "I had never thought of the graph as an art of improvisation, but more as a totally abstract sonic adventure." The often cited line about "liberating the performer" was not found in any source opened.
  • Darmstadt lecture, July 1984 (in Zimmermann ed., Essays, Beginner Press 1985; and in Villars ed., Morton Feldman Says, Hyphen 2006, pp. 191-209): not opened (the pdfcoffee copy returned 403). A search summary attributes to Feldman "I was very interested in this whole problem of meter and the bar line", with "meter and rhythm actually being simultaneous and also being more grid-oriented", reportedly in Claren's essay on For Bunita Marcus; lecture and page not confirmed. [search-confirmed only]
  • Not found anywhere opened: a Feldman remark on 2/2 versus 4/4 as such, on 5/16 by name, or on writing the pitches first and the rhythm later. "The Anxiety of Art" was not opened.

3. The arc inside one composer

  • Graph pieces, 1950-53 (and later): Projection I for cello, boxes = beats at 72 (Williams 1983). Intersection 3 (1953): box = beat at 176, numbers = how many notes (Getty). In the graph "the time is given but not the pitch" (Wolpe essay, 1983). Mine: pitch is the second pass here, made by the player; Tudor's realizations write it out in conventional notation (Getty). Exception: in Intersection 2, "From box 1250 there are five tempo changes" (Hall n. 13).
  • Free durations, 1957-63: Piece for Four Pianos and Two Pianos (1957), *Instrumental Music* (1958; "Durations are free" in its notes, stemless notes, Cline 2019), Last Pieces (1959), Durations I-V (1960-61). In the free-duration pieces "the pitch is given and not the rhythm" (Wolpe essay). For Durations, one program note: "the notes to be played are precisely written but the performers, beginning simultaneously, are free to choose their own durations within a given general tempo" [read, unsigned note, Music We'd Like To Hear, 2019, https://www.musicwedliketohear.com/pdf/01112019.pdf]; Feldman's 1962 liner notes, via Cline, give the effects "like a series of reverberations from an identical sound source" and a "kaleidoscope of sound". On Last Pieces the same program note: "the Last Pieces stipulate no durations at all" and the fourth has "‘durations are free for each hand’". So "Durations" in the title names what the players supply, not what the page holds (mine). Whether Last Pieces noteheads are stemless was not confirmed for that piece (Cline's "stemless" is about Instrumental Music); see for-zach question 7.
  • Precise notation from about 1969-70: Wikipedia (citing a Slee Lecture, 2 Feb 1973): his music "changed radically" in 1970, away from "graphic and arhythmic notation systems in favor of rhythmic precision" [read, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morton_Feldman; the Slee lecture itself not found]. The UE note above ties The Viola in My Life (1970) rhythm to the muted crescendo. Feldman's own reasons: keeping "control of the silence" (1976); precise notation "slows it down" (1982); the graph's "too design-oriented" pages (1983); and denial of a "return" (1982). Rothko Chapel and Madame Press Died Last Week at Ninety (1970) were not looked at.
  • Late re-notated repetition (1977-87): see the table in 5c. Three kinds need keeping apart (mine): (i) same pitches, different rhythm (Triadic Memories mm. 7/13; For Bunita Marcus p. 25): the sound changes; (ii) same pattern under a different meter or bar position (String Quartet 1979 phrase; Triadic Memories mm. 271-272 vs 289-290): the sound may or may not change; (iii) same literal durations, different notation (Thomas's dotted crotchets vs 4:3 minims; Pritchett's quadruplet dotted eighths; Sabat on Piano Piece 1952): the ear gets the same thing and the player does not. Only (iii) is "notation as a chosen second layer" in the strong sense, and it is the least documented by bar number.
  • Counter-cases: the 38 repetitions of the String Quartet No. 2 opening chord keep "registral placement and rhythm" fixed while dynamics vary (Jurkowski, Ex. 3, mm. 1-27).

4. Accidentals: enharmonic respelling

  • Feldman: spelling became a "new concern" around Flute and Orchestra (1977-78), "a sharp is a little more directional", "like adding a little turpentine in the chromatic field", in strings only (Toronto 1982, above). "Spell it one way, then spell it another way" (Middelburg 1985).
  • Marc Sabat, "Shadings in the Chromatic Field: Intonations after Morton Feldman" (MusikTexte 133; text dated 9.1.2004 / 8.5.2012) [read, full text, http://masa.plainsound.org/pdfs/ShadingsED.pdf]:
  • "Starting with Spring of Chosroes (1977), written for violinist Paul Zukofsky, Morton Feldman explored the use of enharmonic spellings in his music, particularly when writing for string instruments."
  • Quotes Zukofsky (Cambridge Companion to the Violin, 1992): "Feldman utilised a system where, for example, an e-flat is played sharper than a d-sharp, or to generalise, for any enharmonic pair, the higher pitch label always implies the higher pitch."
  • "the available evidence clearly states Feldman did not intend his accidentals to imply a Pythagorean tuning."
  • From the 1984 violin Composition manuscript (PSS): "Observe the first bar of his Composition: Bb-G# followed by B#-Gbb!"
  • Ties spelling to rhythm: "Seemingly identical rhythms may be notated in various unorthodox ways, for example—Piano Piece 1952 consists entirely of dotted quarter notes instead of simply quarters, somehow implying a different dynamic shape, an inner division into three parts. As in his notations of rhythm, Feldman plays both with visually distinct notations of “the same pitch” (enharmonic variation) and with visually similar notations of different pitches (chromatic variation)."
  • Thomas (above): G flat in one copy, F sharp in another, "no particular pattern".
  • Hall: the For Philip Guston motto's accidentals and the "Indonesian" joke (above).
  • Not opened: "Note Spelling Without Scales in Morton Feldman's String Quartet No. 2" (Springer chapter, DOI 10.1007/978-3-032-27827-2_18; abstract via search: unusual diminished intervals from accidentals around B-C and E-F); Jürg Henneberger, "Microtonality in Morton Feldman's String Quartet No. 2", in Levy ed., In Search of Morton Feldman (Brepols); Sabat on Composition at New Music USA. [search-confirmed]
  • Mine: the accidentals evidence is stronger than the rhythm evidence for "a later pass", since spellings demonstrably change between copies of one piece (Thomas) and Feldman named spelling as a separate, late-arriving concern (1982). Whether accidentals were added to a pitch draft is still unshown.

5. What this gives the Duration paper (questions and pointers for Zach)

5a. Where "record of procedures" meets "notational image"

  1. Feldman's Volans example writes one figure (four double-dotted notes) in 7/2, 7/4, 7/8 and 7/16. In your terms that is one record of procedures with one more diminution each time. Is that the cleanest "composer angle" example of your diminution procedure in use, and would you want the Volans transcript or Morton Feldman Says as the citation?
  2. Your draft's duration is "that information which can be found when looking at the note itself... notehead, stem, flags and dots". Thomas's two dotted crotchets versus two minims under 4:3 have, he says, "the same literal duration". In your model, is the tuplet bracket part of the note's record or outside it, the way you put tempo outside it?
  3. Feldman says the patterns are "in part notational images that do not make a direct impact on the ear" and "On paper it looks as though it were rhythm. It's not. It's duration." Does your thesis claim the notation is for the reader (performer, analyst) rather than the listener? If so, Feldman is a witness; if not, Donahue's UCSD thesis is the counter-witness (meter as "inflection").
  4. Your Feldman section uses Last Pieces as "a note that we have no opinions about". Feldman used "duration" three ways in the record: what the players choose (Durations), what the page leaves out ("Durations are free"), and what the precise late notation actually is ("It's not. It's duration."). Which one is your note?
  5. Feldman: "rhythm is notation, many times". Does that support "duration as a record of procedures", or does it say something different (that notation is a design act)?
