Setting

Measure every duration in units of one reference note (his "note that we have no opinions about"), and write it in binary. The procedures of his draft then read off the digits:

  • a halving (stem, filled head, flag) shifts the digits one place to the right;
  • the n-th dot adds half of what the dot before it added, so a note of value 2^e with n dots lasts 2^e + 2^(e-1) + ... + 2^(e-n) = 2^(e+1) - 2^(e-n): binary 1s on exactly the positions e-n, ..., e;
  • a tie adds durations, digit by digit, with carries.

Theorem 1 (one notehead)

A duration can be written with a single notehead if and only if its binary digits form one unbroken run of 1s. The notehead is then unique: its value is the highest 1, its number of dots is the length of the run minus one.

Proof. The sum above is a run of 1s. Conversely a run on positions c, ..., a-1 is the note of value 2^(a-1) with a-1-c dots. Binary expansions are unique. ∎

So the single-notehead durations are exactly the numbers whose odd part is 1, 3, 7, 15, ... (one less than a power of two). 3/4 and 7/16 are one notehead; 5/4, 9/8 and 21/16 are not. This is the 2023 abstract's sentence made exact: "The durations which can be notated with a single notehead are limited to a strict subset of rational numbers."

Theorem 2 (ties)

Let a duration have b runs of 1s. It can be written with b tied noteheads (one per run), and with no fewer than (b+1)/2, rounded up. Both ends are reached.

The upper end. One notehead per run, by Theorem 1.

The lower end. Call a transition a place where two adjacent binary digits differ, counting a 0 on either side of the number. A positive number with b runs has exactly 2b transitions. A notehead is a run, 2^a - 2^c: adding one to a tie means adding 2^a and then subtracting 2^c.

Claim: adding or subtracting one power of two changes the number of transitions by at most 2. Adding 2^a when digit a is 0 flips that one digit, and only the two adjacencies touching it can change. When digit a is 1, the 1s from position a up to the first 0 above it (at g) become 0s and that 0 becomes a 1: inside the stretch the digits stay equal to their neighbours, the adjacency between g-1 and g differs before and after, and only the two adjacencies at the ends of the stretch (a-1 | a and g | g+1) can change. Subtracting is the same with 0 and 1 exchanged. So each notehead adds at most 4 transitions. The first notehead alone has exactly 2. Hence k noteheads give at most 4k - 2 transitions, that is at most 2k - 1 runs, and a duration with b runs needs k >= (b+1)/2. ∎

Both ends are reached. 21/16 = 1.0101 has three runs and two noteheads suffice: 15/16 + 6/16, a triple-dotted half tied to a dotted quarter (the carries fill the gaps). In general the alternating pattern 1 0 1 0 ... 1 with 2q+1 ones is the sum of q+1 runs: for 341/256 = 1.01010101 (five runs), 255 + 62 + 24 in 256ths, three noteheads. On the other side, 819/256 = 11.00110011 (three runs, every run and every gap two long) needs all three; the general statement and proof are Theorem C in proof-notes.md.

What this buys, and what it leaves out

  • One page: the single-notehead law, the fact that the obvious tie is not always the shortest, and the exact limit of how much shorter a tie can be (never below half). That is the modest claim; it needs no machinery beyond binary digits and carries.
  • Left for an appendix, if wanted: the exact minimum and the algorithm that finds it (Theorem P), which two-notehead ties exist (Theorem 2 there), and how long gaps limit the saving (Theorem H).
  • The music reading is his to write: flags shift, dots extend the run, ties add with carries; the record of procedures is the binary string.
