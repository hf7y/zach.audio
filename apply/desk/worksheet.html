(a) What each theorem says, in plain English (agent's gloss)

  • Theorem 1. A single notehead (a note value with its flags and dots) can last exactly the numbers that are one unbroken string of 1s in binary, and each such number has only one notehead.
  • Corollary 1a. Ties can reach every duration whose proportion to the reference note is a fraction with a power of two underneath (3/4, 21/16), and nothing else (never 1/3).
  • Theorem B. The obvious tie (one notehead per string of 1s) is never more than about twice as long as the shortest tie, and there are durations where it is that bad.
  • Theorem P. The shortest tie for any duration can be found exactly, by counting how many noteheads cover each binary position and paying only when that count goes up.
  • Corollary S. A shortest tie never needs a value or a dot shorter than the shortest part of the obvious tie, or a notehead longer than its longest.
  • Theorem 2. Exactly the durations of the form "1, a gap, a string of 1s, a single 0, a string of 1s" (and the ones with at most two strings) can be written with two noteheads; 41/32 and 53/32 need three.
  • Theorem C. If every string of 1s and every gap in a duration's binary form is at least two long, the obvious tie is already the shortest.
  • Lemma W. The fewest signed powers of two that add up to a whole number follows a known rule (Reitwiesner's non-adjacent form); it is the tool behind theorem C.
  • Theorem G. Making the gaps longer never guarantees that the obvious tie is shortest: for every gap length there is a duration where a shorter tie exists.
  • Theorem H. Long gaps do limit the saving: if every gap is at least g long, the shortest tie is shorter than the obvious one by at most (shortest minus one) divided by g noteheads.
  • Proposition R. Flags and dots commute on one notehead, but a tie's record is not unique: 5/4 is whole tied to quarter, and also dotted half tied to half.

(d) The practical case, in one paragraph (agent's)

This is a pointer, not a paragraph for your paper. Your question, "what's the practical case?", is answered in writing/duration/practical.md: a tool (bin/tie-min 21/16 prints the binary tie and a shortest tie as LilyPond, with notehead counts) and a count on your own sources and timing tables. The short, honest version: in your own LilyPond files that are not machine-built (77 tie chains in 12 files) the shortest tie never beats the binary tie; in the machine-built scores from the recording-to-score pipeline (378 chains in 5 files) it does in 4 chains (21/16, 21/32 twice, 43/32), and in quantised timings from your own recordings the binary tie is beaten for about 4% to 19% of the durations that need a tie at a 1/32 grid. The counts are upper bounds where a barline cannot be told from the duration, and the dots-bounded case (--max-dots 2) is a search, not a theorem.
