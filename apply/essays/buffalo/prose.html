"Soke" was my first composition to premier at Tulane. I followed a through-composed logic inspired by Toshio Hosokowa. I wrote measure to measure, letting each musical idea inspire the next. I aimed to construct a unified 'meta-instrument' between piano and percussion. Tremolos and small subdivisions are passed between instruments as they cooperate to express each gesture.

I wrote Astrocasper for the Walden summer music festival in New Hampshire. "Casper" refers to the harmonies of Ravel's Gaspard de la nuit. First, I lull the audience into a noisy romantic daydream. Then I break the spell with theater and irreverent quotations of Radiohead and J-Dilla. In Astrocasper, I instruct the players to bow silently, misdirection for the listener. The score is like the Dungeon Master's screen; some arcane secrecy is useful for effect. Astrocasper has the score literally disappear until it's just lines or blank notes.

"Love Me (Long)" melds the timbres of plucked strings and horn together using pulsing rhythm developed in "Soke" percussion. With "Love Me (Long)," I experimented with creating a gradient of dissonance to consonance. I was surprised to discover the effect this had on me as a listener yearning for the hidden beauty to emerge. This piece was inspired by my grandmother who loves Elvis. I have fond memories sitting with her at the piano bench singing and playing songs when I was very young. I quote Elvis in Love Me (Long) and give away the bit at the end. It's the opposite of an inside joke.

With Prof. Ueno, I recognized that composers actively building relationships with performers was composition—just as much as inking the page. Writing for notes on a page, or an abstract instrument, a role, an industrial rendering of a person into a tool, has never satisfied me. That connection is something I can really feel.

I think western notation is the best graphical tool for representing abstract musical ideas. Notation is the mechanism by which I can think on paper. Notation is a layer of judgement, a tool that abstracts with a particular history and ideology behind it. That makes it useful. Notation is programming. Furthermore, it's a useful model of control. How much or how little I notate, how much I specify, how closely I expect performers to adhere to my rules (defer to my authority as an author, really) is a proxy for the power structure that underlies the collaboration. Who is in charge? Who has agency? Improvisation, for example, is a means by which individuals exist as individual agents within a collective practice.

Composing is my practice of spiritual and philosophical contemplation. This inquiry is only complete when it comes to life in my communities. Our open-membership group plays a balance of mid-century folk rock revival and millennial alternative rock. I think a lot about organizations like this, how they often make or break when the founders seek to retire. Can they give up power with the confidence that the vision is protected? How can art bring people together to make more art that brings people together to make art? What about composing for the group as the group?

I'm a magician, and I'm steering. The irony is that the real magic is never what I was trying to perform. In a twist, the works at their best surprise me. And I want to be a neutral steward, but I understand that my craft, my leadership, my magic tricks are needed to initially bend the energy into the closed loop. My hypothesis, the hope, is that one we kick-start the engine, it can hum without me. I know that only when it's humming without me does the real magic happen. The idea is to create works that come alive and continue development out of my hands. It's an ethic. It creates a micro-polity. I have rounded that loop many times already, though it has never outlived me.

I'm investigating how to use a team of professionals to kickstart a community of musicians that sustains itself. The object of study is the anthem which is communication of an identity through performance. The model is a blend of devised work and notated instruction. The workshop period begins with a score played by a professional ensemble. professionals teach amateurs the anthem, then amateurs meet and play the anthem and draft the future music with me. then I exit the loop when notation is produces without my hand once

The first semester tests the recording to score pipeline with me in the loop. I hand build the scores while aligning the mechanism to reproduce the work. The next semester I hand off the scoring and mechanism, training others to run it. The goal is to develop a system that I can cleanly hand off to community members who have become stakeholders, trained on the approach, and with a motivation model that sustains the loop. The goal of year five is that the communication has been modified without me. The final portfolio includes anthems I've written, along with works composed in the course of looping up until the moment of the defense.

I have the insight now of having worked in a church, providing music literally as a "service". I've seen how much music serves. I've seen how rituals facilitate a connectedness to the cosmos, to each other, to the self. These experiences have also led to my belief that composers are therefore responsible for creating the social dynamics we want to see in the broader world. Academically, I want to build on theories to make that potency legible. I want to model for my peers the sense of responsibility I feel.

I'm looking for a community of theorist, support for a longitudinal project, and generally a different environment to refresh my perspectives. I need to figure out what elements of my theory are place specific, and which ones are transposable. Leaving here is essential for putting my hypothesis to the test. I will continue to come back for ritual loops like Carnival.
