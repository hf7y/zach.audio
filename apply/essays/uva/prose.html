In the Krewe of Eris, literacy is questioned, sometimes lampooned. I play the tuba. One of the best players in town Todd Burdock leads our section. He doesn't read; he plays by ear. I hate for my literacy to imply an automatic priority over embodied knowledge. He turned to me, not in submission but in confidence, that I could keep the memory. The technology of notation proves its usefulness, it serves community.

I'm investigating how to use a team of professionals to kickstart a community of musicians that sustains itself. How can art bring people together to make more art that brings people together to make art? The idea is to create works that come alive and continue development out of my hands. Krewe of Vaporwave, Krewe of Eris, the basic Mardi Gras loop is tried and true. I think a lot about organizations like this, how they often make or break when the founders seek to retire. Can they give up power with the confidence that the vision is protected? professionals teach amateurs the anthem, then amateurs meet and play the anthem and draft the future music with me. then I exit the loop when notation is produces without my hand once

I'm interested in things like loops that aren't purely just aesthetic, ritual loops, loops that talk about the passage of time, or that are productive in ways so that things ride on the loop. For example, communities that do charity activity, even though the core loop is recreational, that's the social aid and pleasure club. The pleasure makes the loop. The social aid rides the pleasure. The object of study is the anthem which is communication of an identity through performance. The performance of the anthem models the community's particular guardrails around adaptation. In the parade, my abstention is legible. I can refuse to join a chant, crucially. With the pressing of the music into a record, compression reduces the dynamic range, making my abstention inaudible, my silence is silenced.

Notation is programming. One year I decided to bring in my friend Chiara who played the clarinet in highschool. As a labor of love, I transcribed the woodwind parts. I made a book for just for her, something private and intimate. When the other wind players got word of the book, they started coming to me and asking for copies: something in Eb perhaps. The original compositions aren't played as written. Without institutional memory, the interventions into that text get lost and people default to the authorized version. I created a new snapshot of the present moment. It too will be drifted away from, and that signifies the aliveness of the community.

The model is a blend of devised work and notated instruction. The workshop period begins with a score played by a professional ensemble. The first semester tests the recording to score pipeline with me in the loop. I hand build the scores while aligning the mechanism to reproduce the work. The next semester I hand off the scoring and mechanism, training others to run it. The goal is to develop a system that I can cleanly hand off to community members who have become stakeholders, trained on the approach, and with a motivation model that sustains the loop. The goal of year five is that the communication has been modified without me. A large multimedia work built from fragments of earlier iterations and built on lessons from them, built on relationships with players, venues, a broader community, and a written commentary on successes and failures with theory, all go before the committee

I have rounded that loop many times already, though it has never outlived me. In my opera Siddhartha, I worked with an ensemble of dancers and singers, professionals and amateurs, to develop the vocal and movement material for the second act. The professionals, paid by me, created the opportunity for the amateurs to have the elevated experience. Siddhartha was a good premise, I just didn't close the loop (church sold the building before I could spend my Jazz and Heritage Foundation Grant). These experiences have also led to my belief that composers are therefore responsible for creating the social dynamics we want to see in the broader world.

I received a B.A. in Music from UC Berkeley where I studied digital signal processing and composition. With Prof. Ueno, I recognized that composers actively building relationships with performers was composition—just as much as inking the page. For "Special Pleading," I developed a computer-assisted composition technique. Using PureData, I explored the quarter-tone pitch space. "Tresse" employs computer assisted notation. I used Lilypond's Scheme backend to produce infinitely varied permutations of musical motifs. In my score for "By the time I see you in the morning", I worked with a mixed group of classical and jazz string players to produce an extended timbral palette, combining notation and verbal instruction to suit the players. My piece '67 which I did both with Nouveaux Classical and at UNT in Denton, I used a vocoder patch with a coached ensemble. That's computation in the audio domain. In Film B, I use computation in the notation domain: generate score for players, render score from players. These are loops.

I'm looking for a community of theorist, support for a longitudinal project, and generally a different environment to refresh my perspectives. From New Orleans, I understand that the work should be deeply embedded in community such that the makers and the patrons are all invested collectively in the expression. I need to figure out what elements of my theory are place specific, and which ones are transposable. Leaving here is essential for putting my hypothesis to the test. My current idea is to write anthems and charter many nacent communities, bootstrapping to various intensities based on what initial work accomplishes. I think an earned-revenue non-profit is a good starting place. My bet is on what I know lasts: civic ritual, banding together, social aid and pleasure.
