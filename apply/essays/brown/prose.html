In the Krewe of Eris, literacy is questioned, sometimes lampooned. I play the tuba. One of the best players in town Todd Burdock leads our section. He doesn't read; he plays by ear. I hate for my literacy to imply an automatic priority over embodied knowledge. He turned to me, not in submission but in confidence, that I could keep the memory. It's obviously beautiful when culture is transmitted mouth to ear: intimate. But that's also fragile. And for a founding-era member like Todd, it was hard to feel confident enough to stop shouldering the burden. The technology of notation proves its usefulness, it serves community.

One year I decided to bring in my friend Chiara who played the clarinet in highschool. As a labor of love, I transcribed the woodwind parts. I made a book for just for her, something private and intimate. When the other wind players got word of the book, they started coming to me and asking for copies: something in Eb perhaps. It served the community by opening it up to new amateurs bringing their love to the group. In "Chiasmus" transcribed for Eris, I wrote out the secret Latin choral part, but I whited out the noteheads and the lyrics. The material is still spaced to fit, but learning the music is left as an exercise for the player. You need to go to rehearsal and learn it socially.

Composing is my practice of spiritual and philosophical contemplation. This inquiry is only complete when it comes to life in my communities. I received a B.A. in Music from UC Berkeley where I studied digital signal processing and composition. With Prof. Ueno, I recognized that composers actively building relationships with performers was composition—just as much as inking the page. In my score for "By the time I see you in the morning", I worked with a mixed group of classical and jazz string players to produce an extended timbral palette, combining notation and verbal instruction to suit the players. For "Special Pleading," I developed a computer-assisted composition technique. Using PureData, I explored the quarter-tone pitch space. "Tresse" employs computer assisted notation. I used Lilypond's Scheme backend to produce infinitely varied permutations of musical motifs. I'm working on lily pond extensions right now. My piece '67 which I did both with Nouveaux Classical and at UNT in Denton, I used a vocoder patch with a coached ensemble. That's computation in the audio domain. In Film B, I use computation in the notation domain: generate score for players, render score from players. In Astrocasper, I instruct the players to bow silently, misdirection for the listener.

I do enjoy the founder position and operations engineering, bootstrapping clubs. I am not a cheerleader; I can't fire people up. I do, however, notice where the fire is already burning. Krewe of Vaporwave, Krewe of Eris, the basic Mardi Gras loop is tried and true. I have rounded that loop many times already, though it has never outlived me. In my opera Siddhartha, I worked with an ensemble of dancers and singers, professionals and amateurs, to develop the vocal and movement material for the second act. The professionals, paid by me, created the opportunity for the amateurs to have the elevated experience. Siddhartha does model three structures: orthodoxy (do what's on the page), aesceticism (independent execution of one rule-set held to a personal standard), and hedonism (which is jazz improvisation where the only rule is to have fun). Siddhartha was a good premise, I just didn't close the loop (church sold the building before I could spend my Jazz and Heritage Foundation Grant).

I think a lot about organizations like this, how they often make or break when the founders seek to retire. Can they give up power with the confidence that the vision is protected? How can art bring people together to make more art that brings people together to make art? The insight here is that the senders and receivers are actually the same population or at least of the same population. That's Steven Brown. He mentions how in direct transmission of music, often it's the same people on both sides. In contrast, with the industrial model, senders are specialists and receivers are consumers. What music persuades its recipients to become senders? I'm investigating how to use a team of professionals to kickstart a community of musicians that sustains itself.

The object of study is the anthem which is communication of an identity through performance. In this case the community-specific writing is for the decision making model represented by varying degrees of agency (adaptation) granted to the community members. They take on the identity of being "in" the thing. But being "in" Eris is slippery. It's unsanctioned, unpermitted, anyone can show up and play along. There are no floats. I'm interested in things like loops that aren't purely just aesthetic, ritual loops, loops that talk about the passage of time, or that are productive in ways so that things ride on the loop. For example, communities that do charity activity, even though the core loop is recreational, that's the social aid and pleasure club. The pleasure makes the loop. The social aid rides the pleasure. The idea is to create works that come alive and continue development out of my hands. It's an ethic. It creates a micro-polity. It's an open question whether I can coin a new identity. Right now, my assumption is to align with existing identities. professionals teach amateurs the anthem, then amateurs meet and play the anthem and draft the future music with me. then I exit the loop when notation is produces without my hand once

The performance of the anthem models the community's particular guardrails around adaptation. This is variable: concensus, elective monarchy, anarchy are all plausible principles that may organize the group. I'm not even sure manipulation and care are proper antonyms. There's something about non-compulsion. "Open and voluntary membership" should give individuals the freedom to break from the group. Improvisation, for example, is a means by which individuals exist as individual agents within a collective practice. In the parade, my abstention is legible. I can refuse to join a chant, crucially. With the pressing of the music into a record, compression reduces the dynamic range, making my abstention inaudible, my silence is silenced.

The model is a blend of devised work and notated instruction. The workshop period begins with a score played by a professional ensemble. Notation is the programming. Master players perform the notation. Recipients are community members, guests invited to the open performance. The first semester tests the recording to score pipeline with me in the loop. I hand build the scores while aligning the mechanism to reproduce the work. The next semester I hand off the scoring and mechanism, training others to run it. The goal is to develop a system that I can cleanly hand off to community members who have become stakeholders, trained on the approach, and with a motivation model that sustains the loop. Music is a ritual of obeying instruction. The work requires the practice because notation is the symbology of the input, it's the incantation of control. In fact, the proposal here is almost to model Brown's flow chart as an actual signal flow, in Pure Data, for example, with notation being mediated by a Lilypond layer and a human performance layer. The goal of year five is that the communication has been modified without me. The final portfolio includes anthems I've written, along with works composed in the course of looping up until the moment of the defense.

Academic composers write for a prestige economy. Social and community bands write for their community. I have the insight now of having worked in a church, providing music literally as a "service". I've seen how much music serves. I've seen how rituals facilitate a connectedness to the cosmos, to each other, to the self. These experiences have also led to my belief that composers are therefore responsible for creating the social dynamics we want to see in the broader world. Academically, I want to build on theories to make that potency legible. I want to model for my peers the sense of responsibility I feel.

I'm looking for a community of theorist, support for a longitudinal project, and generally a different environment to refresh my perspectives. From New Orleans, I understand that the work should be deeply embedded in community such that the makers and the patrons are all invested collectively in the expression. I need to figure out what elements of my theory are place specific, and which ones are transposable. If there are lessons here that can be brought elsewhere, it would make the world a better place to do so. Leaving here is essential for putting my hypothesis to the test. I will continue to come back for ritual loops like Carnival.
