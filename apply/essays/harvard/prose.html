I'm investigating how to use a team of professionals to kickstart a community of musicians that sustains itself. How can art bring people together to make more art that brings people together to make art? The idea is to create works that come alive and continue development out of my hands. It's an ethic. It creates a micro-polity. Krewe of Vaporwave, Krewe of Eris, the basic Mardi Gras loop is tried and true. I think a lot about organizations like this, how they often make or break when the founders seek to retire. Can they give up power with the confidence that the vision is protected?

I'm interested in things like loops that aren't purely just aesthetic, ritual loops, loops that talk about the passage of time, or that are productive in ways so that things ride on the loop. For example, communities that do charity activity, even though the core loop is recreational, that's the social aid and pleasure club. The pleasure makes the loop. The social aid rides the pleasure. The object of study is the anthem which is communication of an identity through performance. In this case the community-specific writing is for the decision making model represented by varying degrees of agency (adaptation) granted to the community members. They take on the identity of being "in" the thing. But being "in" Eris is slippery. It's unsanctioned, unpermitted, anyone can show up and play along. There are no floats. It's an open question whether I can coin a new identity. Right now, my assumption is to align with existing identities. professionals teach amateurs the anthem, then amateurs meet and play the anthem and draft the future music with me. then I exit the loop when notation is produces without my hand once

The performance of the anthem models the community's particular guardrails around adaptation. This is variable: concensus, elective monarchy, anarchy are all plausible principles that may organize the group. I'm not even sure manipulation and care are proper antonyms. There's something about non-compulsion. "Open and voluntary membership" should give individuals the freedom to break from the group. In the parade, my abstention is legible. I can refuse to join a chant, crucially. With the pressing of the music into a record, compression reduces the dynamic range, making my abstention inaudible, my silence is silenced.

The model is a blend of devised work and notated instruction. The workshop period begins with a score played by a professional ensemble. The first semester tests the recording to score pipeline with me in the loop. I hand build the scores while aligning the mechanism to reproduce the work. The next semester I hand off the scoring and mechanism, training others to run it. The goal is to develop a system that I can cleanly hand off to community members who have become stakeholders, trained on the approach, and with a motivation model that sustains the loop. The goal of year five is that the communication has been modified without me. A large multimedia work built from fragments of earlier iterations and built on lessons from them, built on relationships with players, venues, a broader community, and a written commentary on successes and failures with theory, all go before the committee

I have rounded that loop many times already, though it has never outlived me. Abe put so much of his uncompensated labor in over ten years that he handed off a thing that's core profitable. Vaporwave makes more money than it costs despite zero founder labor. In my opera Siddhartha, I worked with an ensemble of dancers and singers, professionals and amateurs, to develop the vocal and movement material for the second act. The professionals, paid by me, created the opportunity for the amateurs to have the elevated experience. Siddhartha was a good premise, I just didn't close the loop (church sold the building before I could spend my Jazz and Heritage Foundation Grant). Rick Snow put the grad students in touch with the krewe and we collaborated during Mardi Gras (Oedipus Max).

I received a B.A. in Music from UC Berkeley where I studied digital signal processing and composition. With Prof. Ueno, I recognized that composers actively building relationships with performers was composition—just as much as inking the page. In my score for "By the time I see you in the morning", I worked with a mixed group of classical and jazz string players to produce an extended timbral palette, combining notation and verbal instruction to suit the players. In Astrocasper, I instruct the players to bow silently, misdirection for the listener. My piece '67 which I did both with Nouveaux Classical and at UNT in Denton, I used a vocoder patch with a coached ensemble. That's computation in the audio domain. In Film B, I use computation in the notation domain: generate score for players, render score from players. These are loops. For "Special Pleading," I developed a computer-assisted composition technique. Using PureData, I explored the quarter-tone pitch space.

I'm looking for a community of theorist, support for a longitudinal project, and generally a different environment to refresh my perspectives. From New Orleans, I understand that the work should be deeply embedded in community such that the makers and the patrons are all invested collectively in the expression. I need to figure out what elements of my theory are place specific, and which ones are transposable. My hypothesis, the hope, is that one we kick-start the engine, it can hum without me. Leaving here is essential for putting my hypothesis to the test. I will continue to come back for ritual loops like Carnival. My current idea is to write anthems and charter many nacent communities, bootstrapping to various intensities based on what initial work accomplishes. I think an earned-revenue non-profit is a good starting place. My bet is on what I know lasts: civic ritual, banding together, social aid and pleasure.

In the Krewe of Eris, literacy is questioned, sometimes lampooned. I play the tuba. One of the best players in town Todd Burdock leads our section. He doesn't read; he plays by ear. I hate for my literacy to imply an automatic priority over embodied knowledge. He turned to me, not in submission but in confidence, that I could keep the memory. It's obviously beautiful when culture is transmitted mouth to ear: intimate. But that's also fragile. And for a founding-era member like Todd, it was hard to feel confident enough to stop shouldering the burden. The technology of notation proves its usefulness, it serves community.

One year I decided to bring in my friend Chiara who played the clarinet in highschool. As a labor of love, I transcribed the woodwind parts. I made a book for just for her, something private and intimate. When the other wind players got word of the book, they started coming to me and asking for copies: something in Eb perhaps. It served the community by opening it up to new amateurs bringing their love to the group. In "Chiasmus" transcribed for Eris, I wrote out the secret Latin choral part, but I whited out the noteheads and the lyrics. The material is still spaced to fit, but learning the music is left as an exercise for the player. You need to go to rehearsal and learn it socially.

I have the insight now of having worked in a church, providing music literally as a "service". I've seen how much music serves. I've seen how rituals facilitate a connectedness to the cosmos, to each other, to the self. These experiences have also led to my belief that composers are therefore responsible for creating the social dynamics we want to see in the broader world. Composing is my practice of spiritual and philosophical contemplation. This inquiry is only complete when it comes to life in my communities. Academically, I want to build on theories to make that potency legible. I need to see what's possible outside of here. I would certainly join where I fit in. I would also found one if I felt the opportunity was there (latent energy waiting to get shaped into a loop). I am not a cheerleader; I can't fire people up. I do, however, notice where the fire is already burning. As a leader, my goal is to always be training my replacement which is much easier said than done. As an educator, obviously on some level I know better than my students; that's why they take my classes. But even still, I aspire to not simply tell them what's good; rather, I try to give them the tools to determine for themselves what's good. I want to model for my peers the sense of responsibility I feel. I realize that belonging is an active process, that I have to work to achieve it by building the spaces in which it is possible.
