Target recipients vary based on the context. From first principles, we know that recipients must be technically capable of becoming senders, of producing the message. They can be trained to do so. I imagine friction is least where the least training is required here. If the medium were music, the recipients must include musicians. Recipients of multimedia works can include a greater diversity of talents. Recipients walk out being able to send the message. A concrete example of the loop (this might come from Schoenberg deriding Rossini, source) is the attendee of the opera leaving whistling a theme from an aria. The recipient has become the sender, though with much less sophistication. Whistling happens in the mode of directed stimulation. Directed association that gets stuck in ones head could be a rhyme or a quote or a circumstance or merely a juxtaposition of symbols. That's Rorty's strong poetry at its catchiest. An anthem is another good case study. The Anthem for Eris notably starts where performers may choose their voice number, playing from an <0126> tetrachord. No rules effectively determine who plays what. Sections may confer and count off numbers, but if someone were to switch it would be hardly detectable. In fact the <0126> set is dissonant enough that any "wrong note" would be difficult to hear anyway. Thus the Anthem for Eris embodies the political principles that organize the group itself. Adam Neely's video essay on the US National Anthem show how the presentational and virtuosic properties of the melody reflect the organization of American society into champions rooted for by the masses. Anthems are great examples of music that reproduces itself. The engender a patriotism in the listener that serves a political function. That political functioning produces conditions that reproduce the anthem. Stimulation in the form of epic and triumphal sounds resonate with associations with the nation.

Statement of purpose vs thesis. Not the same document. The statement argues (future tense: what you will do and why here); a research statement documents (past tense); a proposal plans (aims, gap, method, timeline). The thesis paragraph is the spine of the statement, not the statement: faculty guides want the research question to take 40-60% of the statement, with preparation, method and named faculty around it, and the life story kept short. Sources: CMU, Aldrich; Blattman; Brown CoPsy; GradPilot, statement vs proposal; MIT EECS, what faculty look for.

The four tests that recur, applied to §0 as questions.

  1. Important, hard, open (CMU): "explain what is the scientific question, why it is important, why it is hard, and why it is open."
  2. Important: §0 never says why anyone but you needs the answer. Which of your lines carries that? (ans-73 "None of these works demonstrate that" says it is hard, not why it matters.)
