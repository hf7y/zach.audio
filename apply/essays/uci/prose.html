Statement of purpose (956 words)

I'm investigating how to use a team of professionals to kickstart a community of musicians that sustains itself. How can art bring people together to make more art that brings people together to make art? Krewe of Vaporwave, Krewe of Eris, the basic Mardi Gras loop is tried and true. Squeeze up until the big parade day. Explode to drive the piston around the loop and back again from next year. I think a lot about organizations like this, how they often make or break when the founders seek to retire. Can they give up power with the confidence that the vision is protected? If you have to keep kicking, that's a bicycle not a motorcycle.

The pleasure makes the loop. The social aid rides the pleasure. The Anthem for Eris notably starts where performers may choose their voice number, playing from an <0126> tetrachord. No rules effectively determine who plays what. Thus the Anthem for Eris embodies the political principles that organize the group itself. They take on the identity of being "in" the thing. But being "in" Eris is slippery. It's unsanctioned, unpermitted, anyone can show up and play along. It's an open question whether I can coin a new identity. Right now, my assumption is to align with existing identities. professionals teach amateurs the anthem, then amateurs meet and play the anthem and draft the future music with me. then I exit the loop when notation is produces without my hand once

The performance of the anthem models the community's particular guardrails around adaptation. This is variable: concensus, elective monarchy, anarchy are all plausible principles that may organize the group. Identification is a strong force of motivation because exclusion is incredibly painful. So when I'm in a position of power, I'm always wary of how groups form cliques, how insiders posses advantages, and how incumbents may suppress entrants even when that harms the group as a whole. For departure, knowing one can leave and come back is especially important. I'm not even sure manipulation and care are proper antonyms. Care often motivates manipulation.

The model is a blend of devised work and notated instruction. The workshop period begins with a score played by a professional ensemble. Then those musicians with me are going to play for an audience not of listeners, but amateur performers, who will identify with the work and also be able to adapt it. The next week, masters play a "compressed" version of the previous week's material. The first semester tests the recording to score pipeline with me in the loop. I hand build the scores while aligning the mechanism to reproduce the work. The next semester I hand off the scoring and mechanism, training others to run it. I have experimented with some initial pitch to MIDI tools like Ableton's combined with per-instrument microphones or near it. But oral tradition, non-spoken, performance practice layer certainly matters. The goal is to develop a system that I can cleanly hand off to community members who have become stakeholders, trained on the approach, and with a motivation model that sustains the loop. The final portfolio includes anthems I've written, along with works composed in the course of looping up until the moment of the defense.

I have rounded that loop many times already, though it has never outlived me. I have been involved with a group of amateur musicians in New Orleans as a music director since 2020. Our open-membership group plays a balance of mid-century folk rock revival and millennial alternative rock. I'm the master and I solved the modulations, but the group of varying abilities achieved it. In my opera Siddhartha, I worked with an ensemble of dancers and singers, professionals and amateurs, to develop the vocal and movement material for the second act. The professionals, paid by me, created the opportunity for the amateurs to have the elevated experience. Siddhartha was a good premise, I just didn't close the loop (church sold the building before I could spend my Jazz and Heritage Foundation Grant). I only know now about Krewe of Vaporwave how much hidden work was required, that was never surfaced, because now I'm responsible for routing it properly (since I won't just muscle it myself since I consider that a failure). Vaporwave makes more money than it costs despite zero founder labor.

In my score for "By the time I see you in the morning", I worked with a mixed group of classical and jazz string players to produce an extended timbral palette, combining notation and verbal instruction to suit the players. At the least I would credit my players as composers alongside myself. Siddhartha does model three structures: orthodoxy (do what's on the page), aesceticism (independent execution of one rule-set held to a personal standard), and hedonism (which is jazz improvisation where the only rule is to have fun). My piece '67 which I did both with Nouveaux Classical and at UNT in Denton, I used a vocoder patch with a coached ensemble. That's computation in the audio domain. In Film B, I use computation in the notation domain: generate score for players, render score from players. These are loops. For "Special Pleading," I developed a computer-assisted composition technique. Using PureData, I explored the quarter-tone pitch space. "Tresse" employs computer assisted notation. I used Lilypond's Scheme backend to produce infinitely varied permutations of musical motifs.

I'm looking for a community of theorist, support for a longitudinal project, and generally a different environment to refresh my perspectives. I need to figure out what elements of my theory are place specific, and which ones are transposable. Leaving here is essential for putting my hypothesis to the test. I will continue to come back for ritual loops like Carnival.

Personal statement (535 words)

In the Krewe of Eris, literacy is questioned, sometimes lampooned. I play the tuba. One of the best players in town Todd Burdock leads our section. He doesn't read; he plays by ear. The Eris music is chaotic, irregular, atonal at times. He has roughly memorized it over 20 years of playing it, but it's clearly much more difficult to remember than a trad jazz bassline over changes. I hate for my literacy to imply an automatic priority over embodied knowledge. I think often of an essay I read in Ana Sanchez Rojo's Music and Spirituality class. It was about the introduction of printed hymnals in the black church. It cited a poem, from the perspective of an elder singer. Once the culture bearer whom other singers asked to "line out" the hymns, the singer in the poem was now at the bottom of the hierarcy: asking others what the printed music said, what was authorized by an author. Yet, I noticed Todd our elder who like the sousaphone carried the knowledge of our section on his shoulders. He turned to me, not in submission but in confidence, that I could keep the memory. It's obviously beautiful when culture is transmitted mouth to ear: intimate. But that's also fragile. And for a founding-era member like Todd, it was hard to feel confident enough to stop shouldering the burden. The technology of notation proves its usefulness, it serves community.

One year I decided to bring in my friend Chiara who played the clarinet in highschool. As a labor of love, I transcribed the woodwind parts. I transposed from C scores, from noisy and contradictory recordings, and I got handwritten parts with irregular beamings from the section's leaders to engrave. I made a book for just for her, something private and intimate. When the other wind players got word of the book, they started coming to me and asking for copies: something in Eb perhaps. It served the community by opening it up to new amateurs bringing their love to the group. The original compositions aren't played as written. Without institutional memory, the interventions into that text get lost and people default to the authorized version. I created a new snapshot of the present moment. It too will be drifted away from, and that signifies the aliveness of the community.

I received a B.A. in Music from UC Berkeley where I studied digital signal processing and composition. With Prof. Ueno, I recognized that composers actively building relationships with performers was composition—just as much as inking the page. I have the insight now of having worked in a church, providing music literally as a "service". I've seen how much music serves. I've seen how rituals facilitate a connectedness to the cosmos, to each other, to the self. However, over time, I've come to realize that it is not the art or the architecture that creates this feeling of sanctuary, but rather the relationships we build with one another. Academic composers write for a prestige economy. Social and community bands write for their community. Academically, I want to build on theories to make that potency legible. I want to model for my peers the sense of responsibility I feel.

Careers response (74 words)

I do enjoy the founder position and operations engineering, bootstrapping clubs. I think an earned-revenue non-profit is a good starting place. My current idea is to write anthems and charter many nacent communities, bootstrapping to various intensities based on what initial work accomplishes. My bet is on what I know lasts: civic ritual, banding together, social aid and pleasure. The idea is that I can exit the loop both financially and in labor terms.
